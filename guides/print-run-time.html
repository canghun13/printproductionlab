How to Estimate Print Run Time

Separate productive press hours from makeready and downtime before reserving a job on the schedule.

Purpose and production context

Print run time answers a narrow but important question: how long must the press produce impressions after it reaches a stable running condition? That productive block is impressions divided by a sustainable hourly speed, multiplied by the number of separate passes.

A workable production schedule adds fixed and variable non-running time around that block. Keep makeready, plate or color changes, wash-up, proof approval, drying and downstream finishing visible instead of hiding them inside an unexplained speed reduction.

Definitions and distinctions

An impression is one press cycle or image transfer on the basis used by the machine speed. It is not automatically one finished piece: an imposed sheet may carry several pieces, and a duplex or multi-color route may require one or several passes.

A pass is one trip through the press. Count colors only when they create separate passes. A four-unit offset press can print four colors in one pass; a one-color press may need four. Use press sheets rather than saleable pieces when the speed is stated in sheet impressions per hour.

Inputs, units and method

Record impressions per pass, sustainable impressions per hour and sequential passes. Derive impressions from the approved press-sheet plan and production quantity. Use a speed demonstrated on comparable stock, coverage and operator conditions rather than the press nameplate maximum.

Hours per pass = impressions per pass ÷ impressions per hour. Net press hours = hours per pass × passes. Convert the decimal remainder to minutes only for the schedule; preserve the unrounded value in the estimate.

The practical guide Print run-time scheduling value is not the number by itself: it tells the estimator what to reserve, the designer what file geometry to use, or the operator what to verify before release.

Decision checklist

  1. Confirm that quantity is expressed as impressions per pass, not finished pieces.
  2. Verify whether colors, sides, coating or white ink create additional passes on the selected press.
  3. Use a sustainable speed supported by recent production history.
  4. Calculate net press hours, then add makeready and planned non-running allowances separately.
  5. Record the inputs, result and next operation on the quote or production ticket.

Build the scheduled block

Start with net press hours. Add job-level makeready once, then add any per-pass plate, color or wash allowance required by the actual route. Include proof approval, reloads or planned maintenance only when they consume the same reserved press window.

Keep drying and finishing as downstream operations unless they block the press itself. This separation lets the estimator compare actual run speed with estimate later: a job can miss schedule because it ran slowly, because makeready overran, or because the next department was unavailable.

When a due time is fixed, work backward from the finishing handoff. Reserve the productive block and documented allowances, then confirm that stock, plates, proof approval and operator coverage are available before the slot begins.

Common mistakes and cautions

Do not divide by the maximum rated press speed or multiply automatically by the number of inks. Do not mix finished pieces with press-sheet impressions, double-count spoilage already included in the press-sheet quantity, or bury makeready inside a guessed efficiency percentage.

The calculation assumes steady speed. Feeder stops, blanket washes, color approval and operator breaks can change elapsed time. Use actual production history to refine the sustainable speed and fixed allowances for future estimates.
